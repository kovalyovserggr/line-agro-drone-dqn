# 3. Results

## 3.1. Line behaviour and the value of preventive maintenance

The modelled line is strongly maintenance-sensitive. Without preventive maintenance (B0), each wear mode
eventually fails in service. This costs throughput in three ways:

- repair downtime;
- age-related scrap;
- age-related slowdown.

The effect is large in every configuration. Table 4 and Figure 5a compare B0 with the best classical policy B1\*:

| Printers | Throughput, B0 (drones/week) | Throughput, B1\* (drones/week) | Profit gain of B1\* over B0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 25.09 | 27.78 | 11.0% |
| 15 | 31.37 | 34.70 | 10.9% |
| 17 | 34.75 | 37.69 | 8.7% |
| 19 | 34.77 | 37.57 | 8.6% |

<!-- thr_B0_n, thr_B1star_n, gainPM_pct_n -->

Throughput grows with the number of printers up to 17 and then stays flat. Adding the 18th and 19th printer
brings no further output (37.69 vs 37.57 drones/week under B1\*). This agrees with the BOM capacity analysis:

- the loss-free printing limit is 29.4, 36.8, 41.7 and 46.6 drones/week for 12, 15, 17 and 19 printers;
- the extrusion limit is 39.8 drones/week;
- with 17 or more printers, extrusion therefore becomes the bottleneck.

The value of maintenance also grows strongly with degradation intensity. At 15 printers, B1\* earns 5.2%,
10.9% and 23.3% more than B0 in the weak, moderate and strong degradation scenarios (Figure 5b(a)).
<!-- gainPM_pct_weak/moderate/strong -->

The policy question is therefore not *whether* to maintain, but *when* and *where* to maintain. That is where
the policies compared below differ.

## 3.2. Training

Figure 4 [FIG 4: fig_learning] shows the training curves of the throughput-accounting agent DQN-TA in the four
printer configurations. The curves behave alike in all scenarios.

- **PM frequency.** In the first, fully exploratory episode the agents perform about 850 PM interventions per
  26-week episode in the 15-printer scenario. In the last ten episodes they perform about 200.
  <!-- train_pm_first_np15 = 851, train_pm_last10_np15 = 203 -->
- **Profit.** Episode profit rises during the ε-decay phase (first 16 episodes) and is flat afterwards.
- **Agreement between seeds.** In the last ten episodes, the mean episode profits of the three agent seeds
  differ by at most 3.6–11.7 k USD per 26-week episode across the six scenarios.
  <!-- train_last10_spread_* -->
- **Cost.** One agent trains in about 0.5 h of wall time on a single CPU core (0.48–0.64 h).
  <!-- train_wall_h_* -->

Training curves do not reveal policy quality reliably. In the strong-degradation scenario, the three agents'
last-ten-episode training profits differ by only 11.7 k. Yet on the validation seeds agent 2 earned 118 k less
than agent 1 (6 586.5 vs 6 704.8). On the test seeds it lost 85.6 ± 12.5 k against B1\*, while the other two
agents gained about 20 k each.
<!-- sel_str15_a1/a2_val, sel_str15_a2_d -->
Training uses an ε-greedy policy, and evaluation a greedy one. Near-ties in Q-values are resolved differently in
the two regimes, and the difference shows only in a full-horizon greedy evaluation. This motivates the
validation-based agent selection (Section 2.7.3), assessed in Section 3.8.

## 3.3. Main comparison

Table 4 [TABLE 4] reports profit and paired differences to B1\* for all six scenarios. Figure 5 [FIG 5:
fig_scenario_map] shows the printer axis.

**DQN-TA, selected agent.** It is never significantly worse than B1\*. It is significantly better in three of
the six scenarios (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-adjusted p < 0.05):

| Scenario | Δ vs B1\* | p (Holm) |
|---|---|---|
| Moderate, 12 printers | +13.4 ± 3.3 | 0.002 |
| Moderate, 19 printers | +20.7 ± 4.3 | 0.001 |
| Strong degradation, 15 printers | +22.1 ± 3.8 | < 0.001 |
| Moderate, 15 printers | +9.8 ± 3.6 | 0.098 |
| Moderate, 17 printers | +2.3 ± 2.2 | 0.31 |
| Weak degradation, 15 printers | +9.2 ± 5.2 | 0.23 |

Averaged over the six scenarios, the difference is +12.9 ± 3.1 k USD/year. Relative to the profit of B1\*,
the gains are small: 0.03–0.33%.
<!-- T4_pooled_d_SEL, T4_rel_gain_SEL_min/max_pct -->

**DQN-TA, mean of the three agents.** It is at the level of B1\*: +0.6 ± 3.9 on average. It is significantly
better at 12 and 19 printers (+10.0 ± 2.7 and +10.6 ± 2.8), not significantly different elsewhere, and
significantly worse nowhere.

**The classical baselines** are ordered consistently:

- **B1 (single multiplier).** It is worse than B1\* in all six scenarios, significantly in four. Its gap grows
  when extrusion becomes the bottleneck: −9.4 and −6.6 at 12 and 15 printers, but −14.7 and −21.6 at 17 and 19.
  With extrusion as the bottleneck, the extruders need a shorter interval than the printers (B1\* uses m_C1 = 0.35
  vs m_C2 = 0.5–0.75), which a single multiplier cannot provide.
- **B3+ (opportunistic, x = 0.3 on top of m = 0.5).** It is practically identical to B1, at −2.2 to −18.8.
  Its opportunistic component triggers only 1.8–2.8 times per year (Section 3.4).
  <!-- pmwait_B3+_n -->
- **B2 (risk threshold).** It is competitive with B1 when printing is the bottleneck: −13.6 and −17.3 at 12
  and 15 printers. It collapses when extrusion is the bottleneck: −435.9 and −441.1 at 17 and 19 printers,
  and −173.3 under strong degradation.

  The reason is structural. A common risk threshold θ for all classes never triggers PM on the extruders.
  Their per-operation failure probability stays below θ because their operations are short relative to their
  wear scale. Under B2, the OEE of the extruders is 0.847 in every configuration, the same value as without any
  maintenance (B0), while the printer OEE is the highest of all policies (0.887).
  <!-- C1OEE_B2_n, C1OEE_B0_n, C2OEE_B2_n -->
  Neglecting the extruders costs little while they have spare capacity, but it is fatal once they are the
  bottleneck. [CHECK: confirm the per-operation p_f of C1 vs C2 at θ = 10⁻³ in the text of 2.6, or move this

## 3.4. What the agent does differently

To see how the agent's policy differs from B1\*, we re-ran the selected agents and B1\* with the event log on
three test seeds (100000–100002) in three scenarios: 12 and 19 printers with moderate degradation, and 15
printers with strong degradation. Logging does not alter the simulation: all 18 logged runs reproduce the logged
test profits bit for bit. <!-- EV_all_identical, EV_n_runs -->
Figure 7 [FIG 7: fig_pm_age] shows the normalised wear age v/η of the restored component at every PM and at every
wear-mode failure. Figure 8 [FIG 8: fig_timeline] shows the intervention timeline of all extruders and printers
in the 19-printer scenario.

Three differences stand out.

**1. Maintenance effort follows the bottleneck.**

- **12 printers (printing is the bottleneck).** Compared with B1\*, the agent:
  - maintains the printers more often and earlier: 330 vs 288 PM/year, median age at PM 0.176η vs 0.226η;
  - suffers fewer printer failures: 33.7 vs 37.0 per year;
  - maintains the extruders, which have spare capacity, rarely and late: 7 vs 16 PM/year, at a median age of
    0.70η, well beyond the analytical optimum T\*/η = 0.475;
  - still has fewer extruder failures than B1\*: 3.7 vs 5.0 per year.
- **19 printers (extrusion is the bottleneck).** The pattern reverses:
  - the agent maintains the extruders more often and earlier than B1\*: 74 vs 60 PM/year, at 0.125η vs 0.166η;
  - it maintains the printers less often: 246 vs 266 PM/year.

Class-level OEE values agree with this pattern. With 12 printers, printer OEE is 0.882 vs 0.878 and extruder OEE
0.868 vs 0.878; throughput is 27.92 vs 27.78 drones/week. With 19 printers, extruder OEE is 0.895 vs 0.893 and
throughput 37.69 vs 37.57.
<!-- EV_mod12_*, EV_mod19_*, C1OEE_SEL_n, C2OEE_SEL_n, thr_SEL_n -->
This is the behaviour the throughput-accounting reward was designed to induce (Section 2.4.4). It is also what
the local-reward agent fails to do once the bottleneck moves (Section 3.5).

**2. The agent maintains the component's actual age, not the time since the last PM.** B1\* triggers PM when the
operating time since the last PM reaches m·T\*. A corrective repair renews the failed wear component
(Section 2.2.1) but does not reset this counter. Some scheduled PMs therefore fall on a component that was
renewed shortly before. Under B1\*, 7.8–20.7% of PMs restore a component younger than half the policy's median
PM age. The exception is the classes maintained very frequently, where the share is only 2.2–2.5%: the extruders
with 19 printers and the printers under strong degradation. For the agent, which observes the virtual age, the share is 0–0.1%. Its ages at PM are
also more uniform: the coefficient of variation of v/η at PM for the printers is 0.09–0.13 against 0.13–0.29 for
B1\*.
<!-- EV_*_young_pct, EV_*_r_cv -->
In Figure 8 the difference is visible as timing: B1\* maintains all printers (and all extruders) on nearly the
same days, because their operating times run in step, whereas the agent's interventions are spread out
according to each machine's own condition.

This raises a fair question about the baseline. A classical age-replacement policy [REF: Barlow & Proschan 1965]
resets its clock at every renewal, including failure replacement. We therefore evaluate such a variant
(B1a/B1a\*, Section 3.4.1) [TODO Р52: results pending].

**3. Under strong degradation, the agent saves PM cost.** B1\* uses a very short printer interval (m_C2 = 0.25)
and performs 705 printer PMs per year. The agent performs 489, at a later median age (0.151η vs 0.113η). It
accepts slightly more printer failures (36.0 vs 30.7 per year) and comes out ahead on profit (Table 4).
<!-- EV_str15_* -->

**Opportunistic maintenance plays a minor role.** Our initial hypothesis was that a learned policy would schedule
PM while a node waits for inputs, when PM costs little throughput. The agent does this where it is useful: with 12
printers, all of its extruder PMs start while the extruder is blocked or starved.
<!-- EV_mod12_DQN_C1_wait_pct = 100 -->
But extruder PMs are few, so only 1.6–4.0% of all the agent's interventions start during waiting
<!-- pmwait_share_SEL_n -->. The bottleneck class, whose maintenance matters most, rarely waits. Under the decision
grid (Section 2.4.2), a busy node is queried at operation boundaries, because its age grows only during
processing. Consistently with this, the tuned opportunistic rule B3+ gains nothing over periodic PM
(Section 3.3).

## 3.5. Reward design: local versus throughput accounting

Figure 5 (orange markers) and Figure 5a [FIG 5a: fig_ablation17] compare DQN-TA with an otherwise identical
agent trained on the local reward. The local reward credits each node's value added in proportion to its
processing time (Section 2.4.4). The ablation shows that throughput accounting buys robustness to the
bottleneck position rather than a uniformly larger gain:

- **15 printers (printing is the bottleneck).** The local-reward agent is better than B1\* by +16.3 ± 2.5. This
  is at least as good as DQN-TA (mean +3.2, selected +9.8). When printers are the bottleneck, crediting by
  processing time already points maintenance in the right direction: printing dominates the processing time
  of a drone.
- **12 printers.** The local-reward agent is slightly below B1\* (−6.1 ± 3.6).
- **17 printers (extrusion is the bottleneck).** The local-reward agent loses 57.7 ± 2.6 k USD/year. It still
  credits the printers for most of the value, and it maintains the extruders less than either B1\* or DQN-TA:
  - extruder OEE: 0.883 (local reward), 0.889 (DQN-TA), 0.893 (B1\*);
  - printer OEE: 0.878 (local reward), 0.873 (DQN-TA).

  <!-- abl17_* -->
  DQN-TA stays at the level of B1\* in the same scenario.

## 3.6. Degradation intensity

Figure 5b [FIG 5b: fig_degradation] and the last two rows of Table 4 show the degradation axis at 15 printers.

- The value of PM rises from 5.2% to 23.3% of profit (Section 3.1).
- The selected agent's advantage over B1\* rises from +9.2 ± 5.2 (weak) and +9.8 ± 3.6 (moderate), neither
  significant, to +22.1 ± 3.8 (strong, p_Holm < 0.001).
- The mean of three agents shows no advantage (−1.4, +3.2 and −14.4). The strong-scenario mean is pulled down
  by one poor agent, as described in Section 3.2.
- Re-tuning matters for the classical rules as well. Under strong degradation, the best B1 multiplier shifts
  from 0.5 to 0.35, and B1\* uses m_C2 = 0.25.

## 3.7. Robustness to a misspecified failure model

Table 5 [TABLE 5: table_robust_lognormal] and Figure 5c [FIG 5c: fig_robust] report frozen policies on a line
whose wear modes fail according to a lognormal law with the same mean and coefficient of variation.

**Classical rules are robust.** B1 re-tuned on the lognormal truth selects the same multiplier as on the
Weibull model in all three scenarios: m\* = 0.5, 0.5 and 0.35. The frozen B1 is therefore already the
"informed" B1. B1\* remains the best classical rule.

**The agent's advantage survives where maintenance acts on the printing bottleneck, but not elsewhere:**

| Scenario | Selected agent, Δ vs B1\* | Same agent on the Weibull truth | Mean of 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderate, 15 printers | +6.5 ± 3.2 (p = 0.058) | +9.8 | −3.1 |
| Strong degradation, 15 printers | +22.4 ± 2.7 (p < 0.001) | +22.1 | −6.1 |
| Moderate, 17 printers (extrusion bottleneck) | −12.7 ± 3.3 (p = 0.002) | +2.3 | −16.9 |

At 17 printers, the frozen agent loses about as much as B1 (−17.3). In that scenario it performs fewer
interventions than B1\* (365 vs 437 per year) and suffers more failures (26.2 vs 19.4).
<!-- RB_pm / RB_fail mod17 -->
A plausible explanation, not tested here, lies in the observation. The agent's failure-probability feature is
computed from the Weibull model, which keeps the hazard rising. The lognormal hazard peaks and then falls, so
the Weibull feature overstates the urgency of late-life maintenance in a way the agent was never trained to
discount.

The risk-threshold rule B2 fails as on the Weibull truth: −22.8, −442.0 and −159.2.

## 3.8. Sensitivity to unsourced parameters

Table 6 [TABLE 6: table_sens_eta] and Figure 5d [FIG 5d: fig_sens] report the sensitivity study.

**Printer wear scale η_C2, all policies re-tuned and re-trained.** The agent's advantage over B1\* grows with
the speed of bottleneck wear:

| η_C2 | Selected agent, Δ vs B1\* | Mean of 3 | B1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 750 h (fast wear) | +21.8 ± 5.1 (p = 0.001) | +24.8 ± 4.4 (p < 0.001) | −2.6 |
| 1 500 h (base) | +9.8 ± 3.6 | +3.2 ± 2.8 | −6.6 |
| 3 000 h (slow wear) | −0.8 ± 3.6 (p = 0.73) | −4.9 ± 3.3 | −14.4 |

At η_C2 = 750 h all three agents are significantly better than B1\* (+21.8, +23.1 and +29.7).

The re-tuned classical parameters shift as expected:

- the B1 multiplier m\* is 0.5, 0.5 and 0.35;
- B1\* uses (m_C1, m_C2) = (0.75, 0.5), (1.0, 0.5) and (1.0, 0.35);
- the optimal B2 threshold moves from 3·10⁻³ to 10⁻³.

B2 is very sensitive to θ. At η_C2 = 3 000 h, moving θ from 3·10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³ reduces the number of PM
interventions on the tuning seeds about twenty-fold. With a threefold grid, B2 loses 219.1 k USD/year to B1\* on
the test seeds.

**Tooling wear scale η_C4, base policies frozen.** The conclusions are insensitive:

| η_C4 | Selected agent, Δ vs B1\* | Mean of 3 | Profit of B1\* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 500 h | +14.4 ± 3.4 | +7.7 ± 3.4 | 6 869.1 |
| 5 000 h (base) | +9.8 ± 3.6 | +3.2 ± 2.8 | 6 882.5 |
| 10 000 h | +12.6 ± 3.1 | +4.0 ± 3.0 | 6 889.3 |

The assumption on the manual-station tooling therefore does not affect the comparison.

## 3.9. Reliability of validation-based agent selection

In all six main scenarios, the agent selected on three validation seeds was also the best of the three on the
20 test seeds. <!-- sel_match_test_best = 6 -->

Selection matters most where agents differ. In the strong-degradation scenario the rule rejected the poor
agent: validation profit 6 586.5 against 6 704.8 and 6 701.1. That agent loses 85.6 k on the test seeds.

Where agents are nearly equivalent, the rule is neutral. At η_C2 = 750 h the three validation profits were
within 5.7 k of each other (6 612.9, 6 607.6 and 6 607.1), and the selected agent turned out to be the weakest
of the three on the test seeds (+21.8, against +23.1 and +29.7). All three were nevertheless better than B1\*.
<!-- SE_c2eta750_val_a*, SE_c2eta750_test_d_a* -->

Three validation seeds therefore suffice to catch a failed agent, but not to rank agents of similar quality.
The "mean of three" and "selected agent" figures thus bracket what a practitioner can expect from one training
run (lower bound) and from a run with a validation-based choice among three (upper bound).
